# **Chapter 1: Born Between Altars** {#chapter-1:-born-between-altars}

*"Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, before you were born I set you apart"* \- Jeremiah 1:5

---

The acrid sweetness of burning incense filled our living room every morning, carrying whispered prayers to ancestors who watched over us from their mahogany tablets. As Singapore's youngest of eleven children in the mid-1960s, I learned to navigate between worlds before I could fully understand what either one meant—the ancient traditions of my Chinese immigrant family and the rapidly modernizing nation taking shape around us.

Our two-story shophouse in the heart of Singapore bore the sacred geography of traditional Chinese religious life. The ancestral altar dominated our living room, where tablets bearing the names of departed grandparents and great-grandparents stood in hierarchical order, flanked by offerings of oranges, rice, and tea that my mother refreshed daily. Before school each morning, I would watch her light three joss sticks, their smoke rising toward the ceiling as she murmured prayers in Hokkien dialect—entreaties for protection, prosperity, and the continued blessing of those who had crossed to the other side.

This was Singapore in the 1960s: a young nation where 75.9 percent of residents were ethnic Chinese, most carrying forward the religious practices of their homeland even as they adapted to their new maritime home. Our family embodied this complexity perfectly. We practiced the syncretic blend of Buddhism, Taoism, and Confucianism common among overseas Chinese, where spiritual beliefs combined traditional ancestor worship with deities like Mazu, the goddess of the sea, and Guanyin, the goddess of mercy.

Yet beneath these visible devotions lay a mystery that would not reveal its significance until decades later.

## ***The Religious Landscape of 1960s Singapore*** {#the-religious-landscape-of-1960s-singapore}

The Singapore of my childhood was a spiritual crossroads where ancient beliefs met modern uncertainties. According to the 1957 census, the religious landscape reflected our immigrant heritage: 58.8% practiced various forms of Chinese traditional religion (Buddhism, Taoism, ancestor worship), 22.8% were Muslim, 8.6% Christian, 5.4% Hindu, with the remainder following other faiths or claiming no religion.

For Chinese families like ours, religion was not a matter of choosing between competing systems but of participating in a rich tapestry of spiritual practices that had evolved over centuries. Our morning incense honored ancestors according to Confucian filial piety. Our annual temple visits combined Buddhist prayers for merit with Taoist rituals for prosperity. During times of crisis, we might consult mediums who channeled spirits, or seek blessings from various deities depending on our specific needs.

The temples that dotted Singapore's landscape served as community centers as much as places of worship. The Thian Hock Keng Temple, built by Hokkien immigrants like my father, provided not just spiritual services but social networks, cultural education, and mutual aid societies that helped newcomers establish themselves in their adopted homeland. The elaborate festivals—Chinese New Year, Hungry Ghost Festival, Mid-Autumn Festival—created rhythms of celebration and remembrance that connected scattered immigrant communities to their shared heritage.

Christianity, while present, remained largely foreign to most Chinese families. The 8.6% Christian population was concentrated among certain ethnic groups and educational backgrounds, with Presbyterian and Methodist churches serving primarily English-educated Chinese and Eurasian communities. For traditional families like ours, Christianity was associated with Western missionaries and colonial influences—respected perhaps, but not relevant to our daily spiritual needs.

The government's policy of religious harmony, even in these early independence years, meant that spiritual diversity was tolerated and even encouraged as part of Singapore's multicultural identity. Prime Minister Lee Kuan Yew's administration understood that religious stability was essential for social cohesion in our diverse society, but this tolerance also meant that traditional practices continued largely unchallenged by modern alternatives.

## ***The Secret Baptism*** {#the-secret-baptism}

My third sister, closest to me in age and affection, would sometimes tell me stories about our father that seemed too strange to believe. "Ah Pa was baptized in the sea," she would say matter-of-factly, as if discussing what we had eaten for dinner. "Before you were born, when he first came from Fujian."

In those days, many Chinese immigrants from Fujian Province carried complex relationships with both traditional beliefs and Christian influences. Protestant missionaries had been active in coastal China since the early 1800s, with Presbyterian and Methodist churches establishing significant presence in Fujian's port cities. The London Missionary Society, American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions, and Reformed Church in America had all worked extensively among Fujian's Min-speaking population since the 1840s.

By the time my father migrated to Singapore in the 1920s, it was not uncommon for young Chinese men to have encountered Christian teaching, even if they hadn't formally converted. Missionary schools provided education that was often superior to traditional options, and Christian hospitals offered medical care that traditional medicine couldn't match. Some immigrants carried baptismal certificates not from deep conviction but as helpful documentation for accessing Christian social services in their new homelands.

But a sea baptism suggested something more significant than mere convenience. In Chinese Christian tradition, full immersion baptism often marked genuine conversion rather than social accommodation. The symbolism was powerful: just as my father had crossed dangerous waters to reach Singapore, so he had crossed spiritual waters to reach new faith. The sea that had carried him from old life to new life had also carried him from old beliefs to new hope.

Yet this spiritual transformation, if it was genuine, remained largely hidden during his life in our household. Perhaps the demands of supporting eleven children in a Chinese community made open Christianity impractical. Perhaps his wife's strong commitment to ancestral traditions created domestic tensions that silence could avoid. Perhaps he maintained both systems simultaneously, as many Chinese Christians did—honoring ancestors as cultural duty while trusting Jesus for spiritual salvation.

The mystery of his sea baptism would remain unsolved throughout my childhood, surfacing only in my sister's occasional comments and my own wondering about the spiritual currents that had shaped our family's religious waters long before I learned to navigate them myself.

## ***A House of Eleven*** {#a-house-of-eleven}

Our household buzzed with the controlled chaos that characterizes large Chinese families. Seven sisters, three brothers, and me—the youngest, born when my mother was already weary from years of childrearing. In 1960s Singapore, families of this size were becoming less common as government family planning campaigns began influencing reproductive choices, but they remained normal among Chinese immigrant families who saw children as both blessing and security.

The dynamics of an eleven-child household shaped my earliest understanding of hierarchy, responsibility, and survival. By the time I was old enough to remember family life clearly, most of my older siblings had married and established their own households, following the traditional pattern where children married in their early twenties and moved out to start independent families. This meant that I grew up primarily with the siblings closest to my age, while maintaining relationships with married siblings who visited regularly with their own growing families.

The economic pressure of supporting eleven children in 1960s Singapore was immense. My father rose at 4 AM each day to pursue the manual labor that provided our family's income—dock work, construction, and various jobs that demanded physical strength and long hours. The Singapore of the 1960s was rapidly industrializing, creating demand for workers willing to do the hard physical labor that built our nation's infrastructure, but wages remained modest and job security was minimal.

Our shophouse reflected both the constraints and the dignity of working-class Chinese life. The ground floor served multiple functions: living area, dining space, and shrine room where our ancestral altar held place of honor. The upper floor contained bedrooms that had been subdivided and rearranged numerous times to accommodate our changing household composition. Privacy was scarce, personal space was minimal, but family connection was constant and unavoidable.

Meals were communal affairs where conversation flowed in Hokkien dialect, stories were shared, and family business was conducted. The dining table accommodated whoever happened to be present—a number that fluctuated as married children visited with their spouses and children, creating a revolving community of relatives who maintained connection across multiple households.

The older siblings served as surrogate parents for younger ones, with clearly understood responsibilities for supervision, education, and discipline. My immediate older sister, in particular, played a crucial role in my early development, providing the day-to-day guidance and emotional support that our parents couldn't offer while managing the practical demands of such a large household.

## ***First Among Equals*** {#first-among-equals}

At ten years old, something unexpected happened that would shadow the rest of my educational journey: I topped my primary class.

Singapore's education system in the 1960s was still evolving from its colonial legacy while adapting to the needs of an independent, multilingual society. The government's commitment to education as the pathway to economic development meant that even working-class families like ours could access quality schooling, but the system itself was undergoing rapid transformation that created opportunities and challenges in equal measure.

Schools operated in four main language streams—English, Chinese, Malay, and Tamil—with parents choosing based on their aspirations for their children's futures and their own linguistic capabilities. Our family, like most Chinese immigrant families, enrolled me in a Chinese-medium school where instruction occurred in Mandarin, though students also learned basic English as a required second language.

The decision to use Chinese-medium education reflected both practical and cultural considerations. My parents were most comfortable communicating in Hokkien dialect, which was closer to the Min languages that Chinese-medium schools accommodated than to the English that dominated colonial institutions. Chinese schools also provided cultural continuity, teaching Chinese history, literature, and values alongside modern academic subjects.

However, by the 1960s, perceptive parents were already recognizing that English-medium education offered better pathways to economic advancement in Singapore's developing economy. Government jobs, professional careers, and business opportunities increasingly required English fluency, making Chinese-medium education a potential limitation despite its cultural benefits.

I remember little about the mechanics of achieving first place in my class. There were no tutors—such services were beyond our family's means and not yet common in Singapore's educational culture. There was no intensive preparation, no family pressure for academic excellence, no strategic planning about educational achievement. Many Chinese families in the 1960s focused more on basic literacy and respect for teachers than on competitive academic performance.

Yet somehow, in that class of thirty-plus children, I managed to excel across subjects that ranged from Chinese literature to basic mathematics, from moral education to simple science. Perhaps it was natural curiosity, perhaps the advantage of having older siblings who could help with homework, perhaps simply the blessing of intellectual capacity that hadn't yet been tested by serious academic challenges.

The achievement felt both significant and fragile. There was pride, certainly, but also awareness that maintaining such performance would require continued effort and favorable circumstances. Even at ten years old, I sensed that academic success was not automatic but needed to be earned repeatedly through sustained work and good fortune.

More importantly, being first in class created expectations—from teachers, from family members, and from myself—that academic excellence would continue throughout my educational journey. These expectations would prove both motivating and burdensome as circumstances beyond my control began to disrupt the stable foundation that early success required.

## ***A Nation Finding Its Soul*** {#a-nation-finding-its-soul}

The Singapore of my childhood was a country in the midst of profound transformation. The 1960s marked our transition from British colonial rule to self-government (1959) and finally to full independence (1965), with our brief and traumatic separation from Malaysia creating the conditions that would forge our distinctive national identity.

For families like ours, this political evolution created both opportunities and uncertainties. Independence meant that local rather than colonial priorities would shape government policies, potentially creating more opportunities for Chinese immigrants and their children. But it also meant economic uncertainty as Singapore sought to establish its place in the world without the guaranteed markets that British colonial status had provided.

The government's response to these challenges would profoundly influence my generation's life trajectories. Prime Minister Lee Kuan Yew's administration pursued rapid industrialization, universal education, public housing development, and social modernization policies that transformed Singapore from a colonial trading post into a modern nation-state within a single generation.

The public housing program, launched in 1960, was already beginning to reshape Singapore's physical and social landscape. Traditional kampongs and crowded shophouses like ours were gradually being replaced by planned HDB (public housing) estates that provided better living conditions while breaking up traditional ethnic enclaves. By the late 1960s, over 100,000 people had moved into public housing, creating new forms of multiracial community that would become characteristic of modern Singapore.

The education system was expanding rapidly to provide the skilled workforce that industrialization required. Primary school enrollment increased from 83% in 1960 to nearly universal levels by the late 1960s, while secondary and technical education options multiplied to serve students with different aptitudes and career goals. The Bilingual Education Policy, implemented gradually throughout the 1960s, aimed to give all students competency in both English and their mother tongue, preparing them for Singapore's multicultural economy.

Economic development proceeded at breathtaking pace. The Economic Development Board, established in 1961, successfully attracted foreign investment in manufacturing industries that provided jobs for workers like my father while creating opportunities for educated workers in technical and professional fields. GDP grew at double-digit rates throughout the 1960s, creating optimism about Singapore's economic future even during periods of political uncertainty.

For Chinese families, these changes created both continuity and disruption. Traditional cultural practices could be maintained within the broader framework of multicultural Singapore, but economic advancement increasingly required adaptation to English-language institutions and Western business practices. The tension between preserving cultural identity and achieving economic mobility would influence family decisions about education, career choices, and social relationships for decades to come.

## ***The Weight of Expectations*** {#the-weight-of-expectations}

Success, even modest success, creates its own pressures. Being first in my primary class generated expectations from family members who began to speak of my academic achievement with the particular pride that Chinese parents reserve for educational accomplishment. There were subtle suggestions that I might be the sibling who would advance furthest in school, perhaps even reaching secondary education or beyond.

In 1960s Singapore, completing secondary education was still a significant achievement for working-class families. Many students left school after primary education to begin working and contributing to family income. For the youngest child of eleven to show academic promise suggested possibilities that seemed almost too wonderful to contemplate.

My mother would sometimes pause in her morning prayers to cast hopeful glances in my direction. My older siblings, occupied with their own work and family responsibilities, nevertheless took pride in having a brother who excelled in school. There was talk, vague but persistent, about futures that might involve office work rather than manual labor, monthly salaries rather than daily wages.

These expectations felt both encouraging and burdensome. At ten years old, I lacked the emotional vocabulary to articulate the pressure I felt to maintain performance levels that had emerged somewhat mysteriously. The confidence that felt natural during my successful primary year might prove more elusive as academic demands increased and competition intensified.

More troubling, I began to sense that my family's pride in my achievement carried assumptions about my character and capabilities that might not withstand closer scrutiny. What if my academic success was more accident than aptitude? What if the skills that served me well in primary school failed to transfer to more advanced education? What if the expectations I was generating proved impossible to fulfill?

These anxieties remained mostly subconscious, surfacing only as vague unease when adults spoke too confidently about my educational future. But they would prove prophetic when circumstances beyond my control transformed me from a successful student into a struggling survivor.

## ***Between Worlds*** {#between-worlds}

Looking back from the perspective of decades, I can see that my childhood was spent navigating between multiple worlds that seemed to overlap but never quite aligned. There was the world of ancestral tablets and morning incense, where Chinese tradition provided structure and meaning for daily life. There was the world of Singapore's modernizing society, where English proficiency and technical skills opened doors to prosperity. And there was the world of family expectations, where my academic success carried hopes that extended far beyond my individual achievement.

These tensions were common among overseas Chinese families, who struggled to maintain cultural identity while adapting to new national contexts. The challenge was particularly acute for children, who had to learn to function effectively in modern educational and economic systems while preserving connection to ancestral traditions and family values.

Our daily routines embodied these multiple allegiances. We honored ancestors each morning but studied curricula designed to prepare us for Singapore's economic future. We spoke Hokkien at home but learned Mandarin and English at school. We participated in traditional festivals and temple visits but also absorbed the Western cultural influences that accompanied British colonial institutions and American media.

The mystery of my father's sea baptism added another layer to this complexity. If the story was true, it suggested that Christian influences had already touched our family history, even as we maintained traditional Chinese religious practices. Perhaps the ancestral altar and the whispered prayers represented not pure tradition but already-syncretic faith—the kind of blended spirituality that develops when ancient beliefs encounter new circumstances.

I was too young to recognize these tensions intellectually, but I felt them emotionally as a kind of restlessness, a sense that the categories available to me didn't quite capture the fullness of my experience. The boy who excelled in Chinese-medium education while living in an increasingly English-dominated society, who honored ancestors while absorbing modern values, who carried his family's hopes while feeling uncertain about his own capabilities.

## ***The Gathering Storm*** {#the-gathering-storm}

The tranquil rhythm of morning incense and academic success could not last forever. Even as I enjoyed my brief moment at the top of my class, forces were gathering that would soon disrupt everything I thought I understood about myself and my place in the world.

Singapore itself was changing rapidly. The late 1960s and early 1970s would bring major educational reforms, economic restructuring, and social changes that would reshape the landscape of opportunity for families like ours. The comfortable world of Chinese-medium education was already under pressure from policies that favored English-language instruction and technical skills.

More immediately threatening were the personal tragedies that would soon shatter my family's stability and my own confidence. Death was coming to visit our household—not once, but twice—in ways that would expose me to trauma that 1960s Singapore had no framework for understanding or addressing.

The Chinese funeral traditions that had sustained families for generations would soon become my reality rather than my observation. The elaborate rituals designed to honor the dead and comfort the living would instead become sources of additional trauma for a young boy already struggling to understand loss.

But in those final months of innocence, I remained the youngest son who had topped his class, the family's academic hope, the boy who breathed incense-scented air each morning while dreaming of futures that seemed both traditional and modern, both Chinese and Singaporean, both grounded in the past and open to possibility.

The ancestral tablets watched over our living room with the patient attention of those who had seen generations come and go. They had witnessed immigration and adaptation, tradition and transformation, hope and heartbreak. Soon they would witness tragedy that would test whether the spiritual framework that had sustained my family for generations could also sustain a traumatized child who was just beginning to understand the fragility of everything he had taken for granted.

The morning incense would continue to rise toward the ceiling, carrying prayers that would grow more desperate and more essential as the storm clouds gathered. The boy who had been first in his class would soon discover what it meant to be lost in ways that no curriculum had prepared him to navigate.

But even in tragedy, even in trauma, even in the collapse of everything that had seemed certain, God was already at work—planting seeds in ground that seemed too broken to sustain growth, preparing foundations for blessings that would not be visible for decades to come.

The journey from chronos to kairos was about to begin.

# **Chapter 2: When Death Came Calling \- Trauma and Forgotten Promise** {#chapter-2:-when-death-came-calling---trauma-and-forgotten-promise}

*"Even though I walk through the darkest valley, I will fear no evil, for you are with me" \- Psalm 23:4*

---

Death arrived at our household twice within two years, shattering the innocence of a boy who had been first in his class and replacing it with trauma that would shadow my academic performance for the next decade. At ten and eleven years old, I witnessed losses that my young mind had no framework to process, embedded within cultural rituals that added layers of spiritual confusion to already overwhelming grief.

The Singapore of the early 1970s had little understanding of childhood trauma or post-traumatic stress. Mental health was not yet part of our national vocabulary. Children were expected to be resilient, to adapt without complaint, to honor adults' decisions without question. When death visited Chinese families, elaborate traditional ceremonies took precedence over individual psychological needs.

I had no way of knowing that the terror and confusion I experienced during those deaths would reshape my brain's development, creating learning difficulties that would persist until divine intervention unlocked my intellectual potential more than a decade later. I only knew that the confident boy who topped his primary class was disappearing, replaced by someone who struggled with basic tasks and lived in constant fear that death might come calling again.

## **The Sentosa Tragedy** {#the-sentosa-tragedy}

The first death came without warning on what should have been a joyful family outing to Sentosa beach. In the early 1970s, Sentosa was still developing as Singapore's premier resort destination, with beaches that offered working-class families affordable recreation and relief from our increasingly urbanized island home.

Our extended family group included several nieces and nephews close to my own age, making these outings precious opportunities for cousins to bond away from the adult supervision that dominated our daily lives. Among them was my beloved niece—technically my eldest brother's daughter, but raised by my mother due to circumstances that reflected the complexity of Chinese family structures in transitional Singapore.

Her mother had left when she was just a baby, disappearing so completely that even years later, no one knew how to contact her or inform her of significant events in her daughter's life. In practical terms, this made my niece more like a sister to me than a niece, someone who shared my daily routines and occupied a special place in my ten-year-old heart.

The drowning happened suddenly, as drownings always do. One moment she was playing in the water with the rest of us children, the next moment there was panic, shouting, desperate searching. The details remain mercifully blurred in my memory, but the outcome was devastatingly clear: the niece who had been like a sister was gone, claimed by the same sea that was supposed to provide recreation and joy.

What followed was my first encounter with the harsh realities of traditional Chinese burial customs. Because she had been born out of wedlock and her mother was absent, the family elders determined that she could not receive proper funeral rites. According to traditional beliefs, children born under such circumstances could not have ancestral tablets made for them, could not be honored through the usual ceremonies that marked the transition from life to death.

She was simply buried in what the adults called "the wild"—a plain grave with no marker, no tablet, no ongoing ritual recognition. For a ten-year-old boy who loved her, this seemed not just heartless but incomprehensible. How could someone who had shared our meals, our games, our daily life simply disappear from family memory as if she had never existed?

The trauma was compounded by the supernatural beliefs that surrounded such deaths in traditional Chinese culture. Adults whispered about spirits of those who died young and without proper burial, about supernatural consequences that might follow from improper handling of death, about the importance of avoiding contamination from such tragic circumstances.

For a young boy already struggling to understand why someone he loved had suddenly vanished, these whispered fears added layers of spiritual terror to natural grief. Death was not just loss but potentially ongoing supernatural threat, not just absence but possible spiritual danger that required careful navigation through complex ritual requirements.

## **Father's Final Surgery** {#father's-final-surgery}

The second death struck our household just one year later, when my father underwent what was described as "minor surgery" in an era when Singapore's healthcare system was still developing its modern capabilities. In the mid-1970s, medical procedures that would be considered routine today carried significantly higher risks, and post-operative care lacked many of the safeguards that contemporary patients take for granted.

My father had been the steady provider for our household of eleven children, rising at 4 AM each day to pursue the manual labor that supported our large family. He embodied the immigrant work ethic that was transforming Singapore from colonial trading post to modern nation—tireless, uncomplaining, focused entirely on ensuring his children had opportunities he had never enjoyed.

The surgery itself seemed successful initially, but complications developed quickly. Diabetes was diagnosed only after the operation, a condition that might have been managed more effectively with earlier detection and the medical knowledge that would become available in later decades. The combination of surgical stress and undiagnosed diabetes proved devastating for his system.

What began as minor surgery became major crisis, then became tragedy that left our family suddenly without its primary breadwinner and our household without its patriarchal anchor. For an eleven-year-old boy still processing the loss of his beloved niece, his father's death felt like confirmation that stability was an illusion, that the people he depended on could vanish without warning.

But if the loss itself was traumatic, the cultural response was even more overwhelming for a child's developing psyche.

## **Seven Nights of Ritual Terror** {#seven-nights-of-ritual-terror}

Traditional Chinese funeral observances in 1970s Singapore involved elaborate ceremonies designed to honor the deceased and ensure their peaceful transition to the afterlife. For adults who understood the cultural logic behind these rituals, they provided structure and community support during grief. For an eleven-year-old boy already traumatized by his father's sudden death, they became sources of additional terror.

The first requirement was that I wear traditional mourning clothes—white robes that marked me as a grieving son and required specific behaviors throughout the funeral period. For seven consecutive nights, I was expected to participate in ceremonies that involved wailing, crying, and ritualized expressions of grief that felt both mandatory and overwhelming.

Each evening brought hours of structured mourning, with family members and hired mourners creating sounds of anguish that filled our home and spilled into the neighborhood. The professional mourners, in particular, produced wails and cries that seemed designed to summon supernatural attention, their voices rising and falling in patterns that suggested communication with spiritual realms I didn't understand.

The combination of genuine grief for my father and artificial grief required by ritual created internal confusion that my young mind couldn't resolve. I missed my father desperately, but the mandatory emotional displays felt disconnected from my actual feelings. The prescribed wailing seemed to belong to someone else's experience of loss, yet I was required to participate as if it represented my own emotional state.

Most disturbing was the way these ceremonies seemed to invoke spiritual presences that felt threatening rather than comforting. The incense, the chanting, the deliberate summoning of supernatural attention—all of it created an atmosphere where the boundary between the living and the dead seemed dangerously thin.

## **The Altar of Memory and Academic Collapse** {#the-altar-of-memory-and-academic-collapse}

After the seven nights of formal mourning, our household acquired a new spiritual focus that would become a source of ongoing trauma: my father's memorial altar. Traditional Chinese custom required maintaining a shrine where the deceased's presence could be honored through ongoing ritual observance.

The altar dominated our living room, displacing the furniture arrangements that had defined our family space during my father's lifetime. His clothing—shirts and pants laid out as if he might return to wear them—created a visual reminder of absence that felt both sacred and terrifying. Photographs, incense burners, food offerings, and ritual objects transformed our home into a kind of temple where the dead maintained ongoing presence among the living.

My mother's obligation was to pray at this altar every night for forty days, a period of intensive mourning that involved wailing, crying, and ritualized communication with my father's spirit. For an eleven-year-old boy trying to process his own grief while preparing for the Primary School Leaving Examination (PSLE)—Singapore's crucial test that would determine secondary school placement—these nightly ceremonies created an environment of constant emotional upheaval.

The timing of my father's death created additional trauma that would have lasting educational consequences. The PSLE was scheduled for the end of my final primary year—just months after my father's death and during the height of our household's mourning observances.

In 1970s Singapore, the PSLE carried enormous weight for working-class families like ours. Success could open doors to prestigious secondary schools and eventual white-collar employment. Failure meant relegation to less desirable schools and limited future opportunities. The examination represented not just academic assessment but social mobility—the difference between continuing the cycle of manual labor or entering the professional classes that were becoming increasingly important in our modernizing economy.

For a boy who had previously been first in his class, the PSLE should have been an opportunity to demonstrate academic potential and secure admission to excellent secondary schools. Instead, it became a nightmare scenario where personal trauma intersected with educational pressure in ways that undermined my ability to perform at previous levels.

The combination of grief, ritual obligations, and ongoing household disruption made sustained study nearly impossible. The nightly mourning ceremonies meant irregular sleep. The emotional volatility of our home environment made concentration difficult. The spiritual atmosphere created by the memorial altar generated anxiety that interfered with clear thinking.

Most significantly, I had begun experiencing what I now recognize as post-traumatic stress symptoms—hypervigilance, intrusive memories, difficulty sleeping, and physical manifestations of anxiety that included the stomach problems that would plague me for years. These symptoms, entirely unrecognized by 1970s Singapore's educational and medical systems, created learning difficulties that made academic performance increasingly challenging.

## **The Beginning of Academic Decline** {#the-beginning-of-academic-decline}

The PSLE results, while not completely disastrous, marked the beginning of a pattern that would define my educational experience for the next decade: performance that fell far short of earlier promise, struggles with material that should have been manageable, and increasing disconnection from academic achievement that had previously come naturally.

This institutional assessment became a self-fulfilling prophecy in ways that deepened the trauma's educational impact. The boy who had been first in his primary class was now surrounded by institutional messages that academic excellence was beyond his capabilities.

I only knew that tasks that had once been easy now felt difficult, that material that should have been manageable now seemed overwhelming.

The post-traumatic symptoms that interfered with concentration, memory, and emotional regulation were unrecognized and untreated. ## **The Forgotten Excellence Remembered** {#the-forgotten-excellence-remembered}

Forty years would pass before I would receive independent confirmation that my early academic success had been real and significant. Standing in a hawker center, one of those quintessentially Singaporean food courts where the aroma of laksa mingles with the chatter of multiple languages, a woman approached me with the kind of enthusiasm that suggests recognition born of deep memory rather than casual acquaintance.

"Are you Chen Jin Jin?" she asked, using my Chinese name with the confidence of someone who had known me in a different lifetime.

I looked at her more closely, searching for familiar features in a face that had been shaped by four decades of living. Nothing immediately registered, which was not surprising—my memories of primary school classmates had been largely overlaid by the trauma that followed and the academic struggles that dominated my teenage years.

"Yes," I replied cautiously, wondering where this conversation might lead.

Her face lit up with genuine excitement. "I remember you\! You were first in our class, and I was second. I could never forget that."

The words hit me like a revelation. Not just competent, but first in class.

Standing in that hawker center, listening to this classmate recount our shared academic history with such clarity and enthusiasm, I realized how thoroughly I had internalized the narrative of limitation that my post-traumatic academic struggles had created. The boy who had topped his primary class had become so buried under layers of educational underperformance and self-doubt that I had almost forgotten he had ever existed.

This was more than simple memory loss—it was the psychological protection that comes from suppressing painful contrasts. When your current reality involves repeated academic underperformance, it becomes easier to forget that you were once academically successful than to constantly measure your decline against your former achievement.

But here was someone who remembered not just my success, but the specific details that made it meaningful. She wasn't recalling some minor academic accomplishment—she was describing a pattern of excellence that had been significant enough to remain in her memory for four decades. The fact that she remembered being second while I was first suggested that our academic competition had been real, sustained, and meaningful.
